Healthcare Careers for Public Health, Psychology, and Social Work Graduates

You don't need a biology degree to work in healthcare. These careers welcome public health, psychology, sociology, and social work graduates, plus the prerequisites you may need to add.

By Saima Ahmad, MPH
September 26, 2026 · 2 min read

  2. If you studied social work

One of the most common questions I hear is some version of: "I didn't study biology. Is healthcare still an option for me?"

It is. Many of the most people-centered careers in healthcare actively value what you learned in public health, psychology, sociology, or social work: communication, understanding behavior, working with families, and seeing health in the context of people's lives. What you usually need to add is a handful of science prerequisites, not a second degree.

Careers where your background is a strength

Occupational therapist

OTs help people get back to the activities of daily life after injury, illness, or disability, and that includes mental health. Psychology is one of the most common majors among OT applicants. Programs are master's or doctoral level, accredited by ACOTE, and graduates take the NBCOT exam. Typical prerequisites include anatomy, physiology, psychology, and statistics. See the OT route.

Speech-language pathologist

SLPs treat communication and swallowing problems in children and adults. If your bachelor's isn't in communication sciences and disorders, many universities offer post-baccalaureate "leveling" courses that prepare you for the master's. See the SLP route.

Genetic counselor

Genetic counseling blends science with counseling skills, and psychology graduates are welcome as long as they complete genetics and biology coursework. It's a two-year master's program. See the genetic counselor route.

Public health professional

If you already studied public health, an MPH from a CEPH-accredited program opens roles in epidemiology, health education, infection prevention, and program management. Psychology and sociology graduates move into this field too. See the public health route.

Healthcare administrator

Hospitals and clinics need leaders who understand people and systems. Health administration welcomes graduates from almost any major, and a master's in health administration (MHA), public health, or business is the usual next step. See the healthcare administrator route.

Registered nurse

Accelerated BSN programs are designed for people with a bachelor's in any field. They typically take 12 to 18 months, followed by the NCLEX-RN. Nursing also has a large mental health specialty. See the nursing route.

Clinical research coordinator

Clinical trials need people who can explain studies to patients, collect data carefully, and keep everything on schedule. Many coordinators have public health or psychology degrees. See the coordinator route.

If you studied social work

Hospitals, hospices, dialysis centers, and clinics all employ medical social workers, who help patients handle discharge planning, insurance, and family and emotional challenges. That path usually runs through a master's in social work (MSW) from a CSWE-accredited program and state licensure. If you want a different clinical role, occupational therapy, nursing, public health, and health administration are all natural next steps.

The prerequisites you'll probably need

Most graduate health programs publish their prerequisite list. For non-science majors, the usual gaps are:

  • Anatomy and physiology, often with labs
  • General biology and sometimes chemistry
  • Statistics
  • Medical terminology

Community colleges and online post-bacc programs offer these at a lower cost. Check each program's rules on online labs and how recent the courses must be.
